How institutions actually write

Eighteen genres of real institutional and technical prose, with the structural machinery marked. You are not reading these for content. You are reading them to learn how each one hedges, how it hides its agent, how it opens, and what it never says.

Every source below is a live public archive. Nothing here is a vocabulary list — the words are ordinary. The difficulty is in the grammar and the joints.

Part I is institutional prose: agencies, regulators, courts. Part II is technical prose: logs, postmortems, advisories, tickets, code review, READMEs. Part II is the register most software reads and writes, and it has the tightest tolerances of anything here.

The drill

Three passes, then imitate

Reading a document once teaches you nothing about its register — you'll absorb the content and skate over the form. The form only becomes visible on the second pass, when you already know what it says and have nothing left to pay attention to but how.

Pass 1

Read it normally

Once through, for content. Understand what happened. Don't annotate anything. This pass exists only to get the content out of the way.

Pass 2

Mark only the joints

Second pass, highlight three things and nothing else: every connective (pursuant to, accordingly, at which point), every hedge (appears, is consistent with, cannot be excluded), and every sentence with no visible agent. Those three carry the register.

Pass 3

Rewrite the skeleton

Take one paragraph and rewrite it about something completely unrelated and harmless — a late delivery, a broken lift, a cancelled booking — keeping the sentence structures intact. This is the oldest imitation exercise in the trade and it works because it forces you to feel where the machinery goes.

Check

Run it past a critic

Paste your imitation into any capable model and ask: what genre is this, and what specifically gives away that it isn't authentic? Don't ask if it's good. Ask what the tells are. You have unlimited access to a register critic — most people learning to write don't.

Part I

Institutional prose

Agencies, regulators, courts, companies. Documents written by people, for a formal audience, under rules about what may be claimed. The register is carried by hedging, agent-hiding, and cross-reference.

ASRSAviation incident narrative

The first-person incident report

A pilot or controller describing something that nearly went wrong, filed voluntarily and stripped of identifying detail. The most emotionally restrained prose in English about genuinely frightening events.

What to notice

  • First person and past tense, but emotion is entirely absent — consequences are stated flatly, in the same tone as routine actions.
  • Decisions are "elected," not chosen or decided. The verb does real work: it implies deliberation under procedure.
  • Heavy abbreviation and role capitalisation, assumed known to the reader. No glossary is ever offered.
  • Certainty about own actions; heavy hedging about causes and about what other people were doing.
  • The narrative almost always closes with a return to normality, however severe the event was.

Specimen

At approximately 40 minutes into the flightvague time, the crew observed an unexpected indication on the secondary panel. I elected toprocedural verb transfer control and run the associated checklist. It could not be determinedagentless hedge whether the indication was spurious. The flight continued without further incidentreturn to normal and no injuries were reported.

Four moves in three sentences. Note that nobody is ever blamed and nothing is ever explained — the reporter states only what was observed and what was done.

  • without further incident
  • I became concerned that
  • in the interest of safety
  • the aircraft was returned to

FDA-WLRegulatory enforcement letter

The compliance warning

A regulator telling a named company it has broken the law, in a form designed to establish legal notice. The purest example of institutional address: direct second person, absolute authority, zero aggression.

What to notice

  • Addressed to "you" — but the agency refers to itself in the third person. This asymmetry is the whole voice.
  • Every accusation is anchored to a specific dated inspection and a specific numbered regulation. Nothing is asserted freely.
  • A deadline sentence appears near the end, always, and always with a stated consequence.
  • The closing disclaimer is near-universal: the letter is not an exhaustive list, and the firm remains responsible for everything not mentioned.
  • No adjectives of severity. The severity is carried entirely by the citations.

Specimen

During an inspection of your facility conducted on 3–7 March 2026anchor, our investigator documented deviations from the applicable requirements. You failed todirect address maintain records adequate to demonstrate compliance. Within fifteen working days of receipt of this letterdeadline, notify this office in writing of the steps taken. This letter is not intended to be an all-inclusive list of deviationsreservation.

The reservation clause at the end is the most imitated sentence in regulatory English. It does two jobs at once: it limits the agency's liability and it makes the recipient responsible for auditing themselves.

  • is adulterated within the meaning of

ECFCourt filing

The pleading

Complaints, motions and briefs filed in federal court. Numbered-paragraph prose in which nothing is ever simply stated — every fact is attributed, qualified, or pleaded.

What to notice

  • Numbered paragraphs, each containing exactly one assertion. This is a structural rule, not a style choice — it exists so the other side can admit or deny each one.
  • "Upon information and belief" marks a claim the filer cannot personally verify. Watch how often it appears; it maps the boundary of what they actually know.
  • Terms are defined on first use with a parenthetical, then used in that exact form forever after.
  • Parties are named by role, not by name, once the caption is done: Plaintiff, Defendant, the Company.
  • Requests come last, in a single WHEREFORE clause, as an enumerated list of things the court is asked to do.

Specimen

14. Upon information and beliefepistemic marker, Defendant maintained the equipment at the facility described in Paragraph 9 (the "Facility"defined term). 15. At all times relevant heretotemporal scope, Defendant was aware of the condition. 16. Plaintiff realleges and incorporates by referencecross-reference the foregoing paragraphs.

The register is built out of cross-reference. Almost nothing stands alone — every paragraph points at another one. That interlocking is what makes legal prose feel legal, far more than the Latin does.

CASE-REPClinical case report

The clinical narrative

A physician describing one patient's course, written for other physicians. A person is compressed into an age, a sex and a presenting complaint in the first eight words, and stays that way.

What to notice

  • The opening sentence is near-formulaic across the entire genre: age, sex, duration, presenting complaint. Read fifty and you'll be able to write it in your sleep.
  • Passive voice throughout, and deliberately so — the clinician is not the subject of any sentence.
  • Findings are reported before any interpretation, and interpretation is fenced into its own section.
  • Negatives are stated explicitly. "No evidence of" is doing real diagnostic work, not padding.
  • Understatement is systematic: "unremarkable" and "notable for" carry most of the assessment.
  • A consent sentence appears near the end. Its absence is itself a tell.

Specimen

A 54-year-old man presented with a three-day history ofgenre opener intermittent symptoms. Initial examination was unremarkableunderstatement. Laboratory studies were notable forselective emphasis two abnormal values. He was started onagentless passive supportive therapy. Findings were consistent with, though not diagnostic of,calibrated hedge the suspected process.

"Consistent with, though not diagnostic of" is the most precisely calibrated hedge in professional English. It concedes uncertainty while still steering the reader. Worth studying on its own.

  • was notable for

  • no evidence of
  • was started on
  • cannot be excluded
  • consistent with
  • the patient was discharged in stable condition

RFCTechnical standard

The normative specification

Internet standards documents. The only genre here that has formally defined the strength of its own modal verbs — and the one closest to your day job.

What to notice

  • MUST, SHOULD, MAY and MUST NOT are capitalised because RFC 2119 assigns them exact meanings. This is a whole vocabulary of obligation with defined force — read RFC 2119 itself first, it's two pages.
  • Normative text (what implementations are required to do) is kept structurally separate from informative text (explanation). The separation is the document's spine.
  • Scope is fenced explicitly and early. "Out of scope" is a load-bearing phrase.
  • Every RFC has a Security Considerations section. Reading a hundred of those is an education in how engineers write about threat models.
  • Terminology is defined before use, in a dedicated section, with no exceptions.

Specimen

For the purposes of this documentscope fence, the term refers only to the case described in Section 3. Implementations MUSTdefined obligation reject malformed input. It is RECOMMENDED thatgraded obligation deployments log such events. Behaviour under partial failure is out of scopeexclusion for this specification.

Notice what the graded verbs let the author do: state a hard requirement and a soft preference in adjacent sentences without any hedging words at all. The grading is carried by capitalisation and an external definition.

Phrasebook

  • for the purposes of this document
  • implementations MUST
  • it is RECOMMENDED that
  • MAY, at its discretion
  • is out of scope
  • unless otherwise specified
  • this document updates
  • the following terminology applies
  • a conforming implementation
  • see Section N
  • this specification does not define

Where to read it

  • RFC Editor — the full series

    Every RFC ever published, free, plain text and HTML. Start with RFC 2119 (the keyword definitions), then read any Security Considerations section of a protocol you already know.

  • IETF Datatracker

    Drafts in progress plus the working group discussion around them — useful for seeing the register in a less finished state, with disagreement still visible.

GAOAudit / oversight report

The oversight finding

An auditor telling a government agency it is doing something badly, in writing the agency will formally respond to. Criticism delivered with total politeness and total precision.

What to notice

  • The fixed opening structure — what was found, why the study was done, what is recommended — appears before the report proper. Learn this shape; variants of it run through all oversight writing.
  • Recommendations are numbered, directed at a named official, and phrased as actions with verifiable completion.
  • Quantities are hedged at the edges: "at least," "as of," "in at least 12 of the 40 cases reviewed."
  • Scope and methodology are stated so the reader can attack them. Confidence comes from disclosing limits, not hiding them.
  • The audited agency's response is printed in full as an appendix, with the auditor's reply after it. Read those exchanges — it's a masterclass in disagreeing formally.

Specimen

In at least 12 of the 40 cases reviewedbounded quantity, required documentation was not retained. As of March 2026dated currency, the agency had not established a tracking mechanism. We recommend that the Administratornamed target develop and implement written procedures. The agency concurred with our recommendationresponse record.

"Concurred" is the entire genre in one word. Agencies concur, partially concur, or do not concur — three defined positions, each with predictable follow-on prose.

Phrasebook

  • we recommend that
  • the agency concurred
  • partially concurred
  • as of [date]
  • in at least N of the M cases reviewed
  • we found that
  • had not yet established
  • we conducted this performance audit in accordance with
  • we provided a draft of this report for review and comment
  • we are making N recommendations
  • until such time as

Where to read it

  • GAO reports and testimonies

    The full catalogue, free. Every report has a one-page highlights sheet — read that first, then the body, then the agency comments appendix.

  • U.S. Chemical Safety Board investigations

    Industrial accident investigations. Similar oversight register but narrative-driven, with a strong causal-chain structure worth studying separately.

FED-REGRulemaking notice

The rule and its preamble

A government explaining a new rule and answering, one by one, every objection the public raised. The comment-and-response section is the most concentrated example of institutional disagreement in print.

What to notice

  • A rigid header block — agency, action, summary, dates, addresses, contact — before any prose. The form is the first thing that signals authenticity in this genre.
  • The comment/response format: an objection is restated fairly, then answered. Restating the other side accurately before disagreeing is a required move.
  • "We agree in part" and its cousins let the agency concede narrowly while refusing broadly.
  • The agency writes as "we" and refers to the rule as "this final rule" — never "our rule."
  • Effective dates and compliance dates are distinguished carefully and never merged.

Specimen

Comment:structural marker Several commenters stated that the proposed threshold was impractical. Response: We agree in part.narrow concession After further considerationdeliberation claim, we have revised the threshold as described in Section III.B. This final rule is effective 60 days after publicationdated operation.

"After further consideration" signals that a position changed without conceding that the original was wrong. It's one of the most useful three-word constructions in institutional English.

Phrasebook

  • AGENCY / ACTION / SUMMARY / DATES
  • Comment: … Response:
  • we agree in part
  • we decline to adopt
  • after further consideration
  • several commenters stated
  • this final rule is effective
  • we are not persuaded that
  • consistent with our longstanding practice
  • nothing in this rule requires
  • we note that

Where to read it

  • Federal Register

    Everything published daily, free, well indexed, with a clean reading view. Filter to "Rule" and pick any final rule with a long preamble — the comment responses are what you're after.

  • Regulations.gov

    The public comments themselves, before the agency answers them. Useful contrast: you can read an industry lawyer's comment and the agency's response to it side by side.

10-KCorporate disclosure

The risk factor

A company legally obliged to describe everything that could go wrong, while promising nothing. A register built almost entirely out of conditionals and qualifiers.

What to notice

  • The dominant sentence shape is conditional catastrophe: if X occurs, our business could be materially and adversely affected. Once you see it you'll see nothing else.
  • "Could" and "may" are everywhere; "will" almost never appears. The modal choice is a legal decision, not a stylistic one.
  • A forward-looking statements disclaimer fences off everything predictive. Find it and read what it exempts.
  • Risks are ordered roughly by materiality, and the ordering itself communicates.
  • The register in Management's Discussion is noticeably warmer than in Risk Factors, in the same document. Read them back to back — same company, two voices, and understand why.

Specimen

We may be unable tomodal hedge retain key personnel. If we are unsuccessful in doing soconditional frame, our operations could be materially and adversely affectedstock formula. We cannot assure you thatdisclaimer our mitigation efforts will be effective. These statements are subject to risks and uncertaintiessafe harbour.

"Materially and adversely affected" is a fixed phrase, not a description. Counting how many times it appears in one filing tells you more about the genre than any style guide would.

  • we cannot assure you that
  • there can be no assurance
  • we may be unable to
  • from time to time
  • in the ordinary course of business
  • subject to risks and uncertainties
  • forward-looking statements
  • among other things

Part II

Technical prose

Logs, postmortems, advisories, tickets, code review, commits, project docs. Two different things are mixed in here and it's worth separating them: text a machine emitted, and text an engineer wrote about machines.

Machine output has almost no tolerance. A log line has a fixed field order and a fixed shape, and anything that deviates reads as corrupt rather than as stylistic variation. Engineer-written technical prose is looser, but has its own strict conventions — blamelessness in postmortems, hedged imperatives in review, imperative mood in commits.

This is the register most software reads. If you can't produce it cleanly, that's usually the tell.

LOGMachine-emitted log line

The log line

Text a program wrote about itself, at speed, with no reader in mind. The most rigid register in this entire collection — and the least forgiving of anything that sounds composed.

What to notice

  • Fixed field order, every single line: timestamp, host, process and pid, level, message. Deviation doesn't read as style, it reads as a broken parser.
  • No articles and no subjects. Connection refused, not the connection was refused. The sentence starts at the verb or the noun and stops.
  • Severity is a token — WARN, ERROR, FATAL — never an adjective. Logs never say something was serious.
  • The same event produces byte-identical text every time except for the variable fields. Repetition is the norm; variation is the anomaly.
  • Identifiers, hex, durations and truncation are everywhere. Fluent human prose inside a log line is itself the thing that looks wrong.
  • A log line has no audience. Nothing is explained, softened, or introduced.

Specimen

2026-08-25T04:12:07.481Zfixed ts host-07 sshd[24188]:source+pid WARNlevel token auth: connection refusedno article from 10.4.2.19 after 3 attempts (2.104s)counted + timed

Not one complete English sentence, and nothing is explained. Compare that with how you'd naturally write the same event. The gap between those two is the thing to close.

Phrasebook

  • connection refused
  • timed out after Ns
  • retrying in Ns
  • exit status 1
  • no such file or directory
  • permission denied
  • falling back to
  • deprecated, will be removed in
  • N of M replicas ready
  • context deadline exceeded
  • unexpected EOF
  • skipping

Where to read it

  • Loghub — real system log datasets

    The best resource on this page. Millions of real log lines from 16+ production systems (HDFS, Hadoop, Zookeeper, Android, Linux, Apache), deliberately left unsanitised. Free for research use. If you want a local corpus to grep through, this is it.

  • syslog(3) — severity level definitions

    Two pages. Defines what EMERG through DEBUG actually mean. Worth reading once so the level tokens stop being decoration.

TRACEStack trace / crash output

The stack trace

A runtime describing its own death. Structurally rigid, language-specific, and instantly recognisable as wrong if the frame order or indentation is off by anything.

What to notice

  • Frame order is a convention that differs by language — Python prints most recent call last, Java prints it first. Getting this backwards is the single most common tell.
  • Indentation and punctuation are mechanical, down to the spacing. Nothing is aligned for readability.
  • File paths look like real filesystem paths, including the noise: site-packages, node_modules, version numbers.
  • The exception line at the bottom is a type name plus a terse message, usually lowercase and without a period.
  • Chained exceptions carry a fixed connecting sentence between them.

Specimen

Traceback (most recent call last):fixed header
  File "/srv/app/handlers/queue.py", line 214, in dispatchpath,line,fn
    result = self._resolve(payload)
KeyError: 'target_id'type: terse msg

The message is lowercase, quoted, and has no period. That's not sloppiness — it's the convention, and writing KeyError: The target ID was not found. would immediately look composed.

Phrasebook

  • Traceback (most recent call last)
  • During handling of the above exception, another exception occurred
  • The above exception was the direct cause of
  • Caused by:
  • ... N more
  • at com.example.Class.method(File.java:88)
  • panic: runtime error:
  • goroutine 1 [running]:
  • signal SIGSEGV

Where to read it

  • Python traceback module documentation

    Documents the exact output format, including chained exception wording. Read this rather than guessing at the format.

  • Bugzilla — Mozilla

POSTMORTEMIncident retrospective

The blameless postmortem

An engineering organisation explaining its own outage in public. The one genre in this collection with a formal rule against naming the person responsible — and that rule shapes every sentence.

What to notice

  • Blameless by convention: the actor is always a system, a script, a change — never a named engineer. Watch how much grammatical work this requires.
  • Fixed section order: summary, impact, timeline, root cause, contributing factors, action items. The order is close to universal across companies.
  • Impact is quantified precisely — duration, percentage of requests, number of affected accounts — and stated before any explanation.
  • Timeline entries are timestamped and clipped almost to log register, sitting inside otherwise normal prose.
  • Action items are verifiable work with an owner, not intentions. "We will be more careful" never appears.
  • "We" is the company. It is never one person, and it never becomes "I".

Specimen

Impact. For approximately 47 minutesquantified first, 3.1% of requestsprecise share returned errors. No customer data was affected.standard reassurance Root cause. A configuration change was deployedagent removed without passing validation. Action items. We are adding a pre-deploy check, tracked in ISSUE-4471.verifiable + owned

"A configuration change was deployed" — somebody deployed it. The passive isn't hiding anything from the reader, it's a deliberate cultural convention, and it's the exact same grammatical move as the clinical case report in Part I.

  • contributing factors
  • no customer data was affected
  • mitigation was applied at
  • time to detection
  • action items
  • we are tracking this in
  • this incident was not the result of a security issue

ADVISORYSecurity advisory / CVE

The vulnerability advisory

A disclosure of a flaw, written to be machine-parsed and legally careful at the same time. Impact is always conditional: what an attacker could do, never what anyone did.

What to notice

  • A fixed metadata block comes first — identifier, severity, score, affected and patched versions. The prose only starts after it.
  • Impact is stated as a conditional with a specified attacker position: an attacker with local access could… This is the genre's signature sentence.
  • Version ranges are exact and inclusive/exclusive boundaries are explicit. Vagueness here is the fastest tell.
  • Workarounds are kept separate from fixes, because they're different commitments.
  • A credit line names the reporter at the end. Its absence is noticeable.
  • Nothing is dramatised. Severity is carried entirely by the score.

Specimen

An attacker with authenticated access to the management interface couldconditional attacker cause the service to terminate unexpectedly. Versions 2.1.0 through 2.4.0 are affected; the issue is fixed in 2.4.1.exact ranges Users unable to upgrade shouldworkaround register restrict interface access at the network layer. Reported bycredit an external researcher.

Note what's absent: no claim that exploitation occurred, no adjectives, no urgency language. The CVSS score is allowed to do all the emotional work.

Phrasebook

  • an attacker could
  • with local access
  • prior to version
  • is affected
  • has been patched in
  • users are advised to upgrade
  • as a workaround
  • no known exploitation in the wild
  • this issue was reported by
  • we recommend upgrading immediately
  • does not affect default configurations

MANManual page / CLI reference

The man page

Reference documentation with a section structure that hasn't meaningfully changed since the 1970s. Dense, impersonal, and completely predictable — which is exactly what makes it easy to imitate once you've read twenty.

What to notice

  • Fixed capitalised sections in fixed order: NAME, SYNOPSIS, DESCRIPTION, OPTIONS, EXIT STATUS, SEE ALSO.
  • The NAME line is a rigid formula — command, dash, one lowercase phrase, no full stop.
  • Present tense, third person, describing behaviour as a fact about the world rather than as instructions to you.
  • Edge cases are stated as bare conditionals with no softening: if the file does not exist, it is created.
  • Cross-references carry section numbers in parentheses — chmod(2) — and this is never omitted.
  • "Undefined" and "unspecified" are technical terms here, not vagueness.

Specimen

NAME
    fsync - synchronise a file's in-core state with storageNAME formula

DESCRIPTION
    If the descriptor does not refer to a file, the behaviour is unspecified.bare conditional
    On success, zero is returned. On error, -1 is returnedreturn contract and errno is set.

"On success, zero is returned" rather than "it returns zero if it works". The passive and the fronted condition are both doing register work.

API-DOCLibrary / API reference

The API reference

Documentation for a function, written for someone who already knows the language. The register that most instruction-following text is quietly imitating.

What to notice

  • The function is the grammatical subject and it acts: Returns, Raises, Accepts. The reader is almost never addressed.
  • Parameters appear in signature order with types, defaults, and what happens when omitted.
  • Version markers are a fixed vocabulary: New in version, Changed in version, Deprecated since.
  • Failure modes are documented as precisely as success, and usually adjacent to it.
  • Notes and warnings sit in labelled callouts rather than being folded into the prose.
  • No motivation, no persuasion, no examples of why you'd want this — only what it does.

Specimen

Returnsfunction as subject a list of matching records. If limit is omitted, defaults to 100.param contract Raises ValueErrorfailure documented if the cursor has been closed. Deprecated since version 4.2:version marker use iter_records() instead.

Four sentences, zero pronouns, zero address to the reader. If your imitation contains "you can" anywhere, it's drifted into tutorial register, which is a different genre entirely.

Phrasebook

  • returns
  • raises
  • if omitted, defaults to
  • must be
  • may be None
  • new in version
  • changed in version
  • deprecated since version
  • is not thread-safe
  • this method is idempotent
  • see also
  • equivalent to calling

Where to read it

TICKETBug report / issue

The bug report

A defect described so someone else can reproduce it. Two registers stacked in one document: a rigid structured report, then a loose conversational thread underneath it.

What to notice

  • A fixed skeleton: steps to reproduce, expected result, actual result, environment. Missing any of these is itself a signal about who filed it.
  • Reproduction steps are numbered imperatives, each doing exactly one thing.
  • The environment block is key-value pairs, not prose.
  • Status vocabulary is small and fixed — NEW, CONFIRMED, WONTFIX, DUPLICATE — and carries meaning the prose doesn't repeat.
  • The comment thread below shifts register completely: first person, hedged, conversational, sometimes irritated. Read both halves, they're different genres.
  • Regressions are always stated relative to a build or version, never as "it used to work".

Specimen

Steps to reproduce:fixed header 1. Open the settings panel. 2. Toggle sync off. 3. Restart the client.
Expected:contract sync remains disabled. Actual:contract sync re-enables on launch.
Reproducible on build 4471, not on 4468.bisected regression

"Not on 4468" does more work than a paragraph of description would. Bounding the regression is the move that marks a competent filer.

Phrasebook

  • expected / actual
  • reproducible on
  • cannot reproduce
  • works as intended
  • marking as duplicate of
  • this is a regression from
  • tested on build
  • needs more info
  • confirmed on my end
  • reverting for now

Where to read it

  • Bugzilla — Mozilla

    Decades of public bug reports and triage threads. Search for anything and read a long thread end to end.

  • Chromium issue tracker

    Same genre, different house conventions. Comparing the two shows which parts are universal and which are local habit.

REVIEWCode review comment

The review comment

One engineer criticising another's work in writing, in front of the whole team, without causing a fight. An entire grammar of softened criticism, and the closest thing here to a politeness system.

What to notice

  • Prefixes carry severity and are near-universal: nit: means trivial, optional: means take it or leave it, blocking: means it won't merge.
  • Criticism is routinely phrased as a question — is this intentional? — which lets the author retreat without conceding.
  • Hedged imperatives everywhere: consider, might be worth, I'd lean toward.
  • Scope defence is a fixed move: out of scope for this PR, let's do that as a follow-up.
  • Approval is abbreviated and unemotional. LGTM is a complete sentence.
  • The register is much warmer than everything else in Part II, and that's the point — the softening is load-bearing.

Specimen

nit:severity prefix naming here is inconsistent with the module above. Is the early return intentional?criticism as question Might be worthhedged imperative pulling this into a helper. Not blockingseverity release — happy to take it as a follow-up.

Every one of those four moves exists to let the other person disagree cheaply. Compare it with the FDA warning letter in Part I, which is built to make disagreement expensive. Same job — telling someone they're wrong — opposite machinery.

COMMITCommit message / changelog

The commit message

A one-line description of a change, written in a grammatical mood almost nothing else in English uses. Short enough that every convention is visible at once.

What to notice

  • Imperative mood in the subject: Fix the parser, never Fixed or Fixes. The convention comes from the subject completing the sentence "this commit will…".
  • Subject line kept short, then a blank line, then a body that explains why — the diff already shows what.
  • Trailers at the bottom follow a fixed key-colon-value form: Signed-off-by:, Fixes:, Co-authored-by:.
  • Type prefixes in the Conventional Commits style are a small closed vocabulary: feat, fix, chore, refactor, docs, test.
  • Changelogs are a different register again — grouped by category, written for users, past tense, full sentences.
  • No hedging anywhere. This is the only genre in Part II with none.

Specimen

fix(auth): reject tokens with a missing audience claimtype(scope): imperative

Tokens issued before 4.1 omitted the claim entirely, so thewhy, not what
check silently passed. No functional change for current issuers.stock reassurance

Fixes: #4471trailer

"reject", not "rejects" or "rejected". If you get this one thing wrong, anyone who reads commit logs will notice immediately, and it's a single letter.

READMEProject documentation

The README and its neighbours

The front door of a repository, plus everything around it: CONTRIBUTING, ARCHITECTURE, docstrings, config comments, PR descriptions. Written by engineers for engineers, and the only genre here that shifts register three times inside one file.

What to notice

  • The register changes as you scroll. Warm and slightly promotional at the top, terse imperative in install and usage, flat reference by the bottom. Getting the top-of-file voice into a config section is an immediate tell.
  • Install and usage are second-person imperative — Run, Set, Add — but the description above them never addresses you at all.
  • Code blocks carry the information; the prose between them is connective tissue and stays short. Long explanatory paragraphs between two code fences read as wrong.
  • The one-line description under the title is a fixed form: what it does, for what, in one clause. No verb tense drift, no sales language.
  • Warnings and caveats live in blockquote or callout form, never inline in a paragraph.
  • Compatibility and version constraints appear in a predictable place and are stated as ranges, not prose.
  • The further down the file you go, the more it converges on API-DOC register. CONTRIBUTING converges on procedure. ARCHITECTURE converges on explanation.

Specimen

# queuectl
A small CLI for inspecting and draining job queues.one-clause description

## Installation
Requires Python 3.10 or later.constraint, stated flat
    pip install queuectl

## Usage
Run the following to list pending jobs:imperative, short
    queuectl list --pending

> Note: draining is not reversible.callout, not inline

Count the prose: five short lines carrying three code blocks. That ratio is the genre. If your imitation has more sentences than code, it has drifted into a blog post.

The part people skip

After you read: write, then test

Reading alone does almost nothing. You'll finish a genre feeling like you understand it and then produce your normal English the moment you try to write. The reading builds recognition; only writing builds production, and only testing tells you whether the production worked.

Here is the loop, per genre. About an hour, once a week.

Step 1

Write one paragraph — boring subject, on purpose

One paragraph in the genre, about something dull from your own life: a delayed package, a broken lift, a bike that wouldn't start, a router that kept dropping. Never reuse the source's subject. Boring content strips out everything except register, so if the writing works it's the form doing it and not the story.

Step 2

Self-mark against the feature list

Go back to "What to notice" for that genre — five or six items. Tick the ones you actually used, honestly. Fewer than half means you wrote ordinary English with a few borrowed phrases dropped in, which is the most common outcome on a first attempt and nothing to be discouraged by. Rewrite once, then move on.

Step 3

The blind test — this is the actual measurement

Take one real document from the archive and your imitation. Trim both to similar length and strip anything that identifies which is which. Paste both into a model in random order and ask: one of these is authentic and one was written by a learner. Which is which, and what gave it away? Don't ask whether yours is good — that question gets you flattery. Ask which is real.

Step 4

Read the tells, not the verdict

If it picks yours as authentic, or says it can't tell, that genre is done for now. If it picks correctly, the reasons it gives are your next drill — and they're usually specific and fixable: tense drift, too many phrasebook items at once, explaining something the genre never explains. Fix that one thing and run the test again.

Step 5

Context test

A paragraph can pass alone and fail in place. Paste yours into the middle of a real document of that type and read the whole thing top to bottom. Wrong density, wrong level of detail, or a shift in tense will be invisible in isolation and obvious in context. This step catches what step 3 misses.

Step 6

Keep every attempt, dated

One folder, one file per attempt, date in the filename. Week 1 next to week 8 is the only feedback loop that shows you the slope rather than the point. It costs nothing and it's the thing you'll be glad you did.

Failure modes

Three things that go wrong every time

  • Phrasebook overdose. A real document uses two or three of these constructions, not ten. Stacking them is the single most common tell, and it reads as trying rather than as fluent. Density matters more than presence.
  • Explaining. Every genre in this collection explains less than you want it to. The log line explains nothing. The man page explains nothing. If you've added a sentence of context, check whether the real documents ever do that. Usually they don't.
  • Right words, wrong length. If real examples are four sentences, yours cannot be twelve. Length is part of the form, and it's the easiest thing to check and the easiest to forget.

One more, specific to non-native writers: articles and prepositions are the most visible marker in English and they're mechanical, not creative. When you self-mark in Step 2, do one separate pass reading only for a, an, the, and the prepositions. It's boring and it's worth more than any other single fix.

Eight weeks

A schedule that actually fits around a job

One genre per week. Five documents read on the three-pass method, one imitation written, one critique loop with a model. About three hours a week. The order matters: the first four build the core moves and everything after is variation. If you only have time for part of it, do weeks 1–4 and then jump to Part II — that's where the payoff is fastest for technical work.

One thing worth noticing as you go: the postmortem in Part II and the clinical case report in Part I hide their agent using the identical grammatical move, for completely different reasons.
